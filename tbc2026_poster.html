Section I

Topic Background

Genomic foundation models are large AI systems trained on billions of DNA sequences (A, T, G, C) from thousands of species. Just as ChatGPT learned the rules of English by reading the internet, these models learn the "grammar" of genomes—which patterns regulate genes, how mutations affect function, and what makes DNA from a human different from a bacterium.

The core idea is next-token prediction: given a stretch of DNA, predict what nucleotide comes next. By training on trillions of bases, the AI builds a deep internal model of how genomes work—without being explicitly taught biology.

These models can then predict whether a mutation causes disease, design synthetic genes with specific functions, annotate newly sequenced genomes, and transfer knowledge across species—all without running a single wet-lab experiment. Every prediction still requires experimental validation, but the speed of discovery is transformed.

Section IV

Ethical, Legal & Social Issues

🚨

Biosecurity

AI that generates realistic DNA could theoretically design pathogens. The 2024 U.S. Executive Order on AI specifically flagged risks from generative biology. Arc Institute uses responsible-release policies and red-teaming before publication.

⚖️

Genomic Bias

Training data is heavily skewed toward European-ancestry genomes. Models may produce worse predictions for underrepresented populations, risking amplification of existing health disparities in clinical genomics.

🔒

Privacy & Consent

Biobank samples were collected before anyone imagined AI training. Genomic data can re-identify individuals. The Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA) has significant gaps—it does not cover life insurance.

📜

IP & Ownership

If AI designs a novel gene sequence, who holds the patent? Current IP law does not address AI-generated biological material. Regulatory frameworks remain untested.

🌍

Access & Equity

Training a 40B-parameter model requires thousands of GPU-hours—resources only wealthy institutions can afford. Open-sourcing helps, but infrastructure and expertise gaps risk creating a two-tier system in genomic medicine.
